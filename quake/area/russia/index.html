Earthquakes in Russia: biggest, latest and history

5,063 earthquakes of magnitude 5 and above in the USGS catalog since 1904.

The catalog lists 5,063 earthquakes of magnitude 5.0 or more whose place name ends in "Russia", from 1904 to 2026. 723 of them are of magnitude 6 to 6.9, 78 of magnitude 7 to 7.9 and 2 of magnitude 8 or more. The strongest is magnitude 9.0 on 4 November 1952, listed as "89 km ESE of Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky, Russia". The most recent one in the catalog is magnitude 5.0 on 11 November 2026.

The busiest decade is the 1990s, with 982 listed earthquakes. Part of such a difference is real activity (aftershocks of a great earthquake can fill a decade with entries) and part is a matter of instruments: records of older decades are less complete. Since 1996, the catalog averages about 65.2 earthquakes of magnitude 5 or more a year for this area.

5,063 of the earthquakes have a depth in the catalog. 89% of them are shallow (under 70 km, the kind most likely to be felt strongly at the surface), 8% are intermediate (70 to 300 km) and 3% are deep (300 km or more). The depth of an earthquake is as important as its size: a deep earthquake of the same magnitude shakes the surface less than a shallow one.

Common questions

What was the biggest earthquake in Russia?

In the catalog, the strongest earthquake named for Russia is magnitude 9.0 on 4 November 1952 (89 km ESE of Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky, Russia).

How often do earthquakes of magnitude 5 or more happen near Russia?

Since 1996 the catalog has about 65.2 per year with this name (magnitude 5 and above). Earlier decades are less complete.

Are these all the earthquakes there?

No. Only magnitude 5.0 and above are included, and the early part of the catalog is incomplete. Many smaller earthquakes happen that are not listed.

Where does the information come from?

From the USGS earthquake catalog. For the full record, maps and reports go to earthquake.usgs.gov.

Data: U.S. Geological Survey, ANSS ComCat catalog, to 12 November 2026. The area is the last part of the place name USGS gives. Magnitudes are of mixed types and earlier records are less complete. For the real-time list see the live list. This site is independent and not part of the USGS.
