Earthquake depth: shallow, intermediate and deep

Every earthquake starts at a point under the surface, called the focus or hypocentre. The depth of that point is reported in kilometres. The same magnitude shakes the surface much harder when it is shallow than when it is deep, so depth is one of the first things to read.

Three classes

  • Shallow: less than 70 km deep. These are the most common and, for the same magnitude, the most damaging.
  • Intermediate: 70 to 300 km.
  • Deep: 300 km and more, down to about 700 km, the deepest earthquakes known.

In the catalog on this site, 82% of the earthquakes have a depth under 70 km, 14% are intermediate and 4% are deep (among those with a depth recorded). Deep earthquakes happen only inside subducting slabs, where a cold plate sinks into the mantle; this is why places such as Tonga, the Kermadec Islands, Fiji and the Bolivia-Peru region have many deep events.

Why depth in the catalog can look odd

Some depths are listed as exactly 10 km or 33 km. These are often default values used when the data were not enough to fix the depth. Early catalog entries may have no depth at all. Treat depths as approximate.

How to use it

When you read about an earthquake in the news, check the depth next to the magnitude on the live list: a magnitude 6 at 10 km under land can be destructive, while a magnitude 6 at 500 km hardly shakes the surface.
