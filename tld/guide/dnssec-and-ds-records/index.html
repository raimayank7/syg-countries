DNSSEC and DS records

DNSSEC is a set of additions to the Domain Name System that lets a resolver check that an answer is genuine and was not changed on the way. It works as a chain: each zone signs its data, and the parent zone vouches for the key by publishing a DS record (delegation signer).

The chain starts at the root

The root zone is signed. If a TLD is signed too, the root zone holds a DS record for it, which is the link that lets a validating resolver trust the TLD's keys. The TLD then does the same for each signed domain under it. If a TLD has no DS record in the root, the chain stops there, and names under it cannot be validated even if their owners sign them.

What the data show

Of the 1436 active extensions, 1350 have a DS record in the root zone. Counting the first DS record of each, the algorithms are: RSA/SHA-256 (1042), ECDSA P-256/SHA-256 (273), RSA/SHA-512 (28), Ed25519 (3), RSASHA1-NSEC3-SHA1 (3), ECDSA P-384/SHA-384 (1). The numbers are the standard DNSSEC algorithm identifiers: 13 and 14 are elliptic-curve algorithms, 8 and 10 are RSA, and 15 and 16 are the newest curves. Each extension page lists the algorithm it uses.

What to do if you register a name

  • Check whether your extension is signed on its page. If it is, you can ask your registrar to sign your name.
  • Signing is worth doing only if your DNS host and registrar both support it; a broken signature makes a name unreachable for resolvers that validate.
  • DNSSEC checks that an answer is genuine; it does not encrypt anything and does not protect the website itself.
