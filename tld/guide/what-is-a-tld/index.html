What is a top-level domain?

A top-level domain, or TLD, is the last part of a domain name: the "com" in example.com or the "in" in example.co.in. It is also called a domain extension. It sits at the top of the Internet's naming tree, one step below the root.

How the naming tree works

The Domain Name System is a tree. At the top is the root zone. Under the root are the TLDs. Under each TLD are the names that people register (example), and under those, whatever their owners create (www, mail). When you type a web address, your computer asks the root where to find the TLD, asks the TLD where to find the name, and asks the name for the website. The root zone is maintained by IANA, which is a function of ICANN; the list of TLDs on this site is IANA's own record.

Who runs a TLD

Every TLD has a manager, called the registry. The registry keeps the master list of names under it and publishes the data that makes them work. It usually does not sell names itself: companies called registrars sell them to the public. For a country-code TLD, the registry is often a non-profit body or a part of a university or ministry of that country; for generic TLDs, it is a company.

How many are there

IANA lists 1436 active TLDs today: 1126 generic, 309 country-code and a few special ones. The number rose from a few dozen to more than a thousand after ICANN opened the system to new generic extensions in 2012; many of those belong to companies as brands.

Second-level names such as .co.in

An address like example.co.in has two levels under the root: the TLD (.in) and a second level (.co) that the registry of .in has set aside for commercial names. These second levels are not in the root zone; each registry decides its own, and they are not listed here. Open the page of a country's extension and follow the link to its registry to see which second levels it offers.
