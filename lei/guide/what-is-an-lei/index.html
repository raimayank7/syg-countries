What is an LEI?

A Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) is a 20-character code that identifies one legal entity, such as a company, a fund or a government body, in a way that is the same in every country and every database. It was created after the financial crisis of 2008 so that regulators and firms could tell exactly who was on the other side of a financial transaction. The standard is ISO 17442.

Who issues it

The LEI system is run by the Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation (GLEIF), a not-for-profit body set up by the Financial Stability Board. LEIs are issued by accredited organisations called Local Operating Units (LOUs); the entity applies to one of them and pays a fee that the LOU sets. GLEIF collects the records of all LOUs into the Global LEI Index, which anyone can search for free.

What a record contains

Each record gives the legal name of the entity, the address of its registration and headquarters, the legal form, the jurisdiction, the date of first registration and the date the record must next be renewed, the status of the registration (for example issued or lapsed) and the LOU that manages it. Some records also show the parent entities that own the entity, where they have reported them. It is "reference data": it says who an entity is, not whether it is sound or trustworthy.

Why companies have one

In several jurisdictions, rules for financial reporting and trading require firms to quote an LEI for certain transactions. A company may also be asked for one by a bank or counterparty. A company that does not trade financial products does not normally need one; ask the party that requires it which rule applies.

Renewal

An LEI must be renewed regularly (every year in the usual case). If it is not, its status becomes "lapsed": the code still exists but is no longer confirmed as up to date. Use the look-up to see the status of a code.
