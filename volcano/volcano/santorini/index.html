Santorini volcano

367 m

Greece · Shield volcano

Santorini is a shield volcano in Greece (36.40°N, 25.40°E), 367 m (1,204 ft) above sea level. The database gives its status as Historical and the code of its last known eruption period as D2.

Of the 6 volcanoes listed for Greece with a recorded height, it is the 4th highest; the median height there is 374 m.

Among all 1,604 volcanoes with a recorded height it ranks 1326th by height.

Significant events in the NOAA database

NOAA lists 7 significant events for this volcano between 1610 BC and 1926, with an estimated 49 deaths in all; the largest has a Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) of 7. Deaths are estimates and VEI is given where it is known.

Position and surroundings

The volcano lies in the northern hemisphere, about 4,048 km from the Equator. The nearest other volcano in the databases is Kolumbo (Greece), 15 km away; 2 volcanoes are listed within 100 km and 5 within 300 km. Distances are straight-line values worked out from the latitude and longitude in the database, and say nothing about how the volcanoes are connected below ground.

Type and status in context

NOAA classes it as a shield volcano. The database lists 1 volcano of this type in Greece (17% of the volcanoes listed there) and 169 in the world (11% of all 1,609). Its status in the database is "Historical", which 589 of the 1,609 volcanoes carry (37%); the status is the database's own label for the kind of evidence of eruption, and this site reports it as filed. The median height of the 169 volcanoes of this type with a recorded height is 1,377 m, so this one is 1,010 m below it.

Reading the event record

The most recent significant event listed is from 1926, 100 years before the date this page was built (2026). Between the first and the last listed event there are 3,536 years, and the average gap between neighbouring events is about 589 years; gaps like this reflect which events were recorded as significant, and not how often the volcano erupts. The event with the most recorded deaths is that of 23 January 1926, with an estimated 48 deaths. 5 of the events are linked in the database to a tsunami and 2 to an earthquake. Records before the modern era are estimates, so use the numbers as orders of magnitude.
