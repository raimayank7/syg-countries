Exoplanets found by transit: 4,799 planets

How the method works

A transit is a small dip in the light of a star when a planet passes in front of it. Telescopes such as Kepler and TESS watch many stars at once and look for these regular dips. The size of the dip tells the planet's size compared with its star, and the time between dips gives the orbit.

What it has found

The archive lists 4,799 confirmed planets discovered by transit, the first in 2002. The planets found this way that have a measured radius have a median radius of 2.46 Earth radii. The table shows up to 40 of them.

What the transit planets are like

Of the 4,799 planets, 4,798 have a measured radius, 4,775 a measured mass and 4,799 an orbital period in the archive. The median radius is 2.46 Earth radii (smallest 0.31, largest 29.3). The median orbit takes 7.97 days. The median distance is 1,492 light years and the farthest is 27,723. By the simple size classes used on this site they are: 2,118 Neptune-like planets, 1,133 super-Earths, 1,002 gas giants, 546 Earth-sized planets. They orbit 3,686 different stars; 734 of those stars have more than one planet in this list. The size classes are a rough sort by radius or mass; the archive records only what was measured, so planets without a measured size are listed as plain planets.

74.2% of all the confirmed planets in the archive were found this way. The busiest year was 2016, with 1,432 planets, and the facilities credited with most are Kepler (2,762), Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) (1,022), K2 (549). A method finds some kinds of planets better than others, so the mix of sizes and orbits above is a property of the method as well as of the planets.
