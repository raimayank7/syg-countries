The habitable zone explained

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with a suitable atmosphere could have liquid water on its surface. It is a place to look, not a verdict: being inside it does not mean that a planet has water, air or life.

Why it depends on the star

A dim, cool star warms a planet only if it is close; a bright, hot star lets a planet be far away. So the zone is close in for small red stars and far out for bright ones. In our own system it begins somewhere near Venus's orbit and ends somewhere near Mars's, depending on the model used, which is why different papers give different limits.

How this site handles it

This site does not draw habitable zones. It uses one simple, plain measure: how much starlight a planet gets compared with Earth (its "insolation"), and its size. The page Small planets that get Earth-like starlight lists planets under twice Earth's radius that receive 0.25 to 1.5 times Earth's starlight: 45 planets in the archive meet this rule.

What is missing

Nothing in the archive says whether such a planet has an atmosphere, how thick it is, or whether the surface is rock or ocean. These need telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope and future missions.
