How exoplanets are found

No telescope can show a planet around another star in the way a camera shows the Moon: the planet is a million to a billion times fainter than its star and almost touches it in the sky. Astronomers find planets by effects they cause. The 6470 planets in the archive were found in a small number of ways.

Transit (4,799 planets)

A transit is a small dip in the light of a star when a planet passes in front of it. Telescopes such as Kepler and TESS watch many stars at once and look for these regular dips. The size of the dip tells the planet's size compared with its star, and the time between dips gives the orbit.

Radial Velocity (1,205 planets)

A planet pulls its star slightly, so the star wobbles toward and away from us. The wobble shifts the colours of the star's light by a tiny amount, which spectrographs can measure. It gives the planet's orbit and a minimum mass.

Microlensing (294 planets)

When a star passes in front of a more distant star, gravity bends and brightens the light of the background star. A planet around the nearer star adds a short extra blip. It is a one-time event that cannot be repeated, but it can find planets far from their stars.

Imaging (98 planets)

Direct imaging blocks the glare of a star and photographs the planet itself. It works best for young, hot planets that are large and far from their stars.

Why most planets are close to their stars

Both main methods favour planets that orbit close to their star: close planets block starlight more often and tug the star harder. The shortest orbits in the archive last only hours. Far fewer planets with orbits like Jupiter's or Earth's have been found, not because they are rare but because they are harder to detect.

The Kepler telescope found 2,787 planets and TESS 1,026 so far in this archive; see Kepler.
