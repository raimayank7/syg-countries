Lookalike characters and normalization

Two pieces of text can be identical to the eye and still differ for a computer. There are two main reasons: different characters that look alike, and the same letter written in different ways.

Lookalike letters

Many alphabets share letter shapes. The Latin small letter a (U+0061, a) and the Cyrillic small letter a (U+0430, а) are two different characters that look the same in most fonts, and so are the Greek small letter omicron (U+03BF, ο) and the Latin o. In a web address or a username this can be used to impersonate: a page can claim to be a known site while using one Cyrillic letter. Browsers guard against this by showing suspicious internationalised domain names in their encoded form, but you should still take care with links in messages.

Look-alikes within one script exist too: the digit 1, the small letter l, the capital I and the vertical bar; or the digit 0 and the capital O. Programmers use fonts that distinguish them.

One letter, two spellings

Unicode can write é in two ways: as a single character, U+00E9 (é), or as the letter e followed by the combining acute accent U+0301 (é). Both look the same, and Unicode says they mean the same text, but their code points differ, so a plain comparison says they are not equal and a search for one does not find the other. The same applies to many letters in Hindi, Punjabi and other scripts that are made of a base letter and marks, and to Korean syllables.

Normalization

Unicode defines normalization forms to make the two spellings equal. NFC joins parts into single characters where possible (é as U+00E9). NFD splits into base and marks. The forms NFKC and NFKD also replace compatibility variants, so "ﬁ" (a ligature) becomes "fi" and the superscript "²" becomes "2", which loses information, so use them only for searching and matching.

In JavaScript: a.normalize("NFC") === b.normalize("NFC"). In Python: unicodedata.normalize("NFC", s). Normalise both sides before comparing or storing names, and use NFC for text you store, as the web and most systems do. macOS file names, for historic reasons, can come out in an NFD-like form, which is the reason a file name can fail to match when moved between systems.

Case-insensitive search

Lower-casing is not enough either: the German ß, the Turkish dotted and dotless i and the Greek final sigma all behave differently. Use the case folding or collation features of your language or database, and test with names from the languages your users speak.
